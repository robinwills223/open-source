# Before You Build: Validating Problem Reality

## Purpose & Core Mindset

The most common failure mode for early-stage software is building a working product that nobody actually needs or uses. Before writing application code, investing in hosting, or hiring contractors, you must determine whether the underlying problem is real and severe enough for people to care.

---

## 1. Rule-of-Thumb Validation Thresholds

Before writing software, aim to satisfy these empirical indicators:

1. **Speak with 10–15 target users:** Conduct structured 20-minute interviews with people who directly experience the problem.
2. **Find at least 3 people who have already spent time or money:** Look for individuals or businesses who have already purchased software, built complex spreadsheets, or hired virtual assistants to cope with the problem.
3. **Consistent pain description:** At least 5 interviewees should independently describe the exact same workflow bottleneck without prompt or suggestion.

> [!TIP]
> If people are not actively trying to solve the problem with existing tools (even clunky spreadsheets), the problem is usually a minor annoyance rather than a painful business priority.

---

## 2. Evidence of Real Demand vs. False Positives

| Signal Type | What It Looks Like | What It Actually Means |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **False Positive** | *"That sounds like a great idea! I would definitely use that."* | Polite encouragement. Requires zero effort or commitment from the listener. |
| **False Positive** | *"My friend would love this; let me introduce you."* | Passing the buck. The person speaking does not have the problem themselves. |
| **Weak Evidence** | *"Send me the link when it's live."* | Low friction dismissal. |
| **Strong Evidence** | *"Here is the spreadsheet we built last month; it takes us 4 hours every Friday."* | Proven past behavior, documented time/money expenditure. |
| **Strong Evidence** | *"Can I pay you right now or put down a deposit to get this sooner?"* | Concrete financial commitment. |

---

## 3. When to Stop (Signs to Pivot or Walk Away)

Do not hesitate to abandon or adjust an idea early. Walking away after 10 interviews saves months of wasted coding. Stop or pivot if you encounter:

- **Apathy:** After describing their workflow, interviewees shrug or say *"It's annoying, but we just deal with it once a month."*
- **Budget Vacuum:** Target users love the concept but have zero discretionary budget or authority to approve a \$10/month tool.
- **Unreachable Audience:** You cannot get 10 target users to respond to an email or direct message. If you cannot reach them for a free interview, you will not be able to sell to them later.
- **Fragmented Workflows:** Every single person handles the process in a completely idiosyncratic way that cannot be generalized into software.
